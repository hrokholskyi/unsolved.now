title: "We still can't explain how ordinary chemistry turned itself into life"
field: chemistry
statement: "How did lifeless molecules on the early Earth organize themselves into self-replicating, metabolizing chemistry that we would call alive?"
first_posed: 1924
posed_by: "Alexander Oparin and J. B. S. Haldane, with the primordial-soup hypothesis"
summary: "Every living thing runs on a tightly coordinated network of proteins, nucleic acids, and metabolism, but no one knows how that network bootstrapped itself from simple prebiotic chemistry. A central puzzle is the chicken-and-egg problem: today's DNA needs proteins to copy it, yet those proteins are built from DNA instructions. The leading 'RNA world' idea proposes that RNA — which can both store information and act as a catalyst — came first, but a fully demonstrated chemical path from geochemistry to a self-sustaining living system does not exist. With no clean origin date, the modern framing traces to the Oparin–Haldane hypothesis of the 1920s and has been sharpened ever since."
why_it_matters: "Answering it would tell us whether life is a near-inevitable consequence of chemistry or a fantastically improbable accident, which directly shapes how likely we are to find life elsewhere. It also underpins synthetic biology and our ability to define what 'alive' even means."
progress:
  - "1924 — Oparin (and Haldane in 1929) propose that life self-organized from a 'primordial soup' of organic molecules"
  - "1953 — the Miller–Urey experiment shows amino acids form from simple gases and an electric spark"
  - "1986 — Walter Gilbert coins the term 'RNA world' for a stage where RNA both stored information and catalyzed reactions"
  - "2025 — review literature reframes the open question as the still-unexplained transition from chemistry to information-bearing biology"
